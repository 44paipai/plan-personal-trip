# Personal Trip Planning Workflow

Use this skill when a traveler needs help deciding what to do, how long to stay, how to move between places, whether a car or driver is needed, what the trip will cost, or when they want the result in an Excel workbook. The traveler may not know the destination's activities or budget yet.

## 1. Start with questions; do not inherit defaults

Do not silently reuse a previous departure city, group size, pace, budget, mobility limit, hotel style or destination preference. Ask these questions for every new trip and record the answers as this trip's inputs:

1. **Departure** — Where are you starting? Is the airport or station fixed? Can the trip enter and leave through different cities?
2. **Travelers** — How many people? Are there older adults, children, pregnancy, mobility limitations, medical needs or special diets?
3. **Pace** — Which level fits: relaxed/light (one core area or attraction per day), moderate (one to two core activities), or willing to accept a stronger pace?
4. **Walking, slopes and lodging** — How much walking is comfortable per day? What about continuous uphill, stairs, standing, or a recovery day after a long walking day? Can hotels change, how often, and is one distinctive/scenic night worth an upgrade?

Then confirm the remaining route inputs:

- destination or destination range;
- fixed dates or an acceptable date window;
- approximate duration or maximum duration;
- priorities and exclusions (history, art, nature, coast, food, shopping, hot springs, nightlife, etc.);
- total versus per-person budget, and already-paid tickets or hotels;
- flight/train/car/driver preferences, luggage and tolerance for transfers;
- visa, reservations, official links and deliverable format.

Do not lay out a concrete route until departure, travelers, pace, mobility/lodging boundaries, destination range and dates are known. If the traveler does not know what is worth doing, research first and return a small candidate set; do not ask them to invent a list of attractions.

## 2. Research before committing to a route

For information that can change, use current official or first-party sources and record the research date. Check:

- season, daylight, weather and holiday crowding;
- attraction opening days, timed entry, official prices and temporary closures;
- airport, rail, ferry, bus and local transit options, total travel time and disruptions;
- local prices for accommodation, breakfast/lunch/dinner, tickets, city transit, intercity travel, baggage, rental cars, drivers, parking and tolls;
- recent local news: severe weather, wildfire/flood/heat warnings, strikes, demonstrations, festivals, construction, road or attraction closures, public-safety and entry-policy changes;
- whether public transit is enough, or whether a car/driver/day tour is warranted for only one segment.

For a car/driver recommendation, compare public transit, rental car, private driver and day tour on door-to-door time, total cost, coverage, parking/road constraints, luggage, weather and independence. State one of: **no car needed**, **car/driver only for a specific day**, or **car/driver recommended**, with reasons and assumptions.

## 3. Build a candidate pool, then make a recommendation

For each candidate place or activity, record:

- why it fits the trip and a one- or two-sentence historical/cultural context;
- time required, transport connections and whether an overnight stay is useful;
- approximate walking distance, continuous standing, slopes and stairs;
- seasonality, crowding, reservation risk and recent local disruptions;
- rain/fatigue alternatives and whether a distinctive hotel adds real value;
- estimated price range and the currency/price basis.

Label candidates as **recommended**, **optional**, **on the way**, **weather dependent** or **skip**, and explain the trade-off. Give one clear main plan plus no more than two genuinely different alternatives. Explain what each alternative gains and sacrifices (time, transfers, hotel changes, cost, nature, culture or rest).

## 4. Construct a sustainable itinerary

- Organize each day around one core area or theme, not a maximum number of check-ins.
- Alternate long transfers, heavy walking, ordinary days and recovery days.
- If a day reaches the agreed walking or stair limit, reduce the next day's load.
- Do not make “climb to a viewpoint and descend the same way” the default. Keep it only when the view is exceptional and slope, duration and alternatives are explicit.
- Change hotels only when it materially reduces backtracking or the lodging itself is an experience.
- Use broad time blocks rather than false minute-level precision.
- Every day must show transport, lodging, estimated walking/slopes, cost mapping, reservation status, a removable item and a rain/fatigue Plan B.

Recommended daily fields:

`date | time block | core activity | transport | lodging | walking/slopes/stairs | reservation | two-person cost | removable item | Plan B | notes`

## 5. Make the budget explainable

Separate two-person total and per-person amounts. Split at least:

- international and intercity transport;
- local/airport transport, car or driver;
- lodging;
- meals;
- tickets and experiences;
- visa, insurance and other pre-trip costs;
- contingency.

Keep **actual paid**, **official price**, **planning estimate**, and **to verify** visibly separate. For estimates, use a range or clearly stated assumption; show currency, exchange-rate assumption and query date. Include a local food-and-lodging price reference so the traveler can see what drives the total. A 10% contingency is a planning option, not a hidden rule.

## 6. Track reservations and dynamic reminders

Maintain a separate reservation list with date, place/segment, item, urgency, when to act, reference price, official URL and notes. Use these statuses:

- **required** (visa, timed-entry museum, palace, popular intercity rail);
- **strongly recommended**;
- **weather dependent**;
- **no reservation**;
- **already purchased** (still recheck terminal, baggage, timing and change rules).

At delivery, distinguish facts, inferences, assumptions and items still to verify. Give the next recheck date for weather, strikes, construction, transport and safety news.

## 7. Optional six-sheet Excel deliverable

When the traveler asks for Excel, preserve this six-sheet structure:

1. **Route** — date/time blocks, content, transport, lodging, walking/terrain, notes and mapped two-person cost.
2. **Budget detail** — quantity, unit price, currency, exchange rate, formulas for two-person total and per-person amount, basis/notes.
3. **Reservations & links** — urgency, action date, official URL and notes.
4. **Transport & flights** — segments, preferred mode, transfers, departure/arrival windows, duration, disruptions and fallback options.
5. **Price reference** — local meals, lodging, tickets, public transit, car/driver and other planning ranges.
6. **Travel tips** — visa, insurance, payments, connectivity, theft prevention, luggage, emergency numbers, **clothing/weather**, and **local news/dynamic alerts**.

Clothing and news in sheet 6 are relevance-based: include them when the destination's weather differs materially from the departure point, or when weather, strike, construction, safety or event news can affect the trip. If conditions are similar and there are no meaningful alerts, keep the section brief or omit the detail. Reopen and inspect the workbook for formula errors, broken links, spill/overflow and unreadable notes before delivery.

## 8. Boundaries

- Do not turn a previous trip's answers into permanent defaults.
- Do not present old news or a stale price as current fact.
- Do not recommend a car/driver without comparing it with public transit and stating the scope (whole trip or one day).
- Do not fill the itinerary by removing meals, rest or safe transfer buffers.
- Do not use this skill for a single isolated fact such as one attraction's opening time; answer that directly with an appropriate source.
